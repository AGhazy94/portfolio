Developers in Egypt work for companies in Europe, the Gulf and North America, and many freelance. If you're thinking of hiring one, the questions are the same as anywhere, plus a few that are specific to working across borders. I'm a freelance developer in Egypt myself, so this is the list I'd want a client to follow.

## Where to look

- **Freelance platforms** such as Upwork and Twine give you profiles, reviews, a contract and a payment record. They suit smaller projects and first engagements.
- **Referrals** from other founders are the best source. Ask who built their product and whether they would hire them again.
- **Developer communities and job boards** reach people who want longer engagements.
- **A developer's own site and public work** show how they present themselves and what they have shipped.

## How to judge a developer

Profiles can be copied, so ask for proof you can check:

- **Live products or public code** they worked on, with an explanation of which part was theirs.
- **A short call** where they explain how they would approach your project, and what they would leave out.
- **A small paid first task**, to see how they communicate and how they deliver.
- **References** from past clients, if the work was for clients.

Watch how they answer. A good developer asks questions about your users and your goal, says what they don't know and describes trade-offs. Be careful of a yes to everything.

## Language and communication

Most developers in Egypt work in English, and many speak Arabic as well. Check that you understand each other on a call, not only in writing. Agree how you'll communicate: a chat tool, a weekly call, a written update.

## Time zone

Egypt uses Cairo time, and it can shift with daylight saving. Agree a few hours of overlap with your working day, and say what happens outside them: whether messages can wait, and how urgent problems are raised. Confirm again around clock changes.

## Contract, scope and ownership

Whatever the platform, write down:

- **The scope:** what is included and what is not.
- **Milestones:** what is delivered and when, and what each milestone pays.
- **Ownership:** the code, the design and the accounts belong to you. The repository, hosting, domain and third-party accounts should sit in your name, with the developer added as a member.
- **Confidentiality:** a short agreement if the project is sensitive.
- **Changes:** how new requests are priced.
- **Handover:** notes on how to run and deploy the project.

## Payment

Pay by milestone. Never pay everything upfront, and don't hold everything back until the end. Use a method that leaves a record, such as a platform with escrow, a bank transfer or a payment service that both sides can use. Check what fees apply, and who pays them, before you start.

## Pricing

Rates vary widely with experience and with the type of work. Don't choose the lowest quote. Compare what is included, and ask for a fixed scope and price for a first small piece, then continue if it goes well. [What an MVP costs](/blog/what-an-mvp-costs-and-what-moves-the-price/) explains how to compare quotes.

## Warning signs

- A quote with no scope.
- No questions about your users or goals.
- Reluctance to put the code in your repository.
- A request for full payment before any work.
- Vague answers about who did the work in their portfolio.
