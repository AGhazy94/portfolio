A freelance developer, an agency and a fractional CTO solve different problems for a first product:

- **Freelance developer:** best when the scope is clear and small, and you can make product decisions yourself.
- **Agency:** best when you need several skills at once, such as design, development, testing and project management, and you want one team to be accountable.
- **Fractional CTO:** best when you need technical judgement more than hands on the keyboard.

If you have an idea, some budget and no technical co-founder, all three come up in every conversation, and the wrong one is expensive to undo once the work has started. I'm a freelance product engineer and full-stack developer, so I'm not neutral. The last section says where I'm the wrong choice.

## Freelance developer

A freelancer is good at building a defined thing quickly. One person knows the whole code, you talk to them directly, and there's less overhead than with a team.

The weak spot is that the same person is often the only one who knows the system. If they get ill or leave, progress stops, and if you can't judge their work, you won't know there's a problem until late. So ask for a repository and hosting in your name, deployments you can see each week, and written notes on how to run the app.

## Agency

An agency suits bigger scopes and fixed deadlines, where design, development and testing have to happen together and you want one company to answer for it.

It's a poor fit for a small project or one whose scope keeps changing. You pay for coordination, and the people in the sales meeting are often not the people building.

I've been on the agency side. Through caisy in 2023 I was one of four developers on Constellatio, a learning platform for law students in Germany. Badges, interface work, the learning games and payments moved at the same time, and we reviewed each other's pull requests. I merged 71 of the 151. One freelancer can't run four streams of work at once.

Before you sign with an agency, ask for the names of the people who will do the work and what happens to the code and accounts at the end.

## Fractional CTO

A fractional CTO works part-time, usually a few days a month, and acts as your technical lead. They're there for decisions: which technology or vendor to pick, whether to hire an engineer, how to check someone else's code.

It breaks down when you expect them to build everything. Many fractional CTOs manage the technical side and don't write the code, so the build still needs someone else.

Before you sign, ask what they will personally do each month, and how they handle a developer who disagrees with them.

## How to choose

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Do I know exactly what to build?** If yes, a freelance developer or an agency can price it. If no, start with a short scoping exercise, or a fractional CTO's advice.
2. **Can I judge the technical work?** If not, find someone who can review it, whether that is a fractional CTO, an advisor or a trusted friend with engineering experience.
3. **How much coordination can I take on?** A freelancer needs a clear owner on your side. An agency can bring its own project manager, at a price.

## Combinations that work

- **Freelancer plus a reviewer.** A freelance developer builds, and a technical advisor reviews the plan and the pull requests once or twice a month.
- **Agency for design, freelancer for the product.** The agency makes the design system, and a developer builds the product from it.
- **Fractional CTO, then a hire.** The CTO shapes the plan and the first hire, and the engineer takes it from there.

## What a product engineer adds

A product engineer is a developer who also takes on the product questions: what the first version has to prove, which feature can wait, and whether a release made things better for the people using it. One person still writes the code front to back, as with a freelance developer, but you get help with the decisions as well as the typing.

That suits a first product where you know the problem but not yet every feature. It doesn't replace an agency when you need a full team, or a fractional CTO when the questions are about hiring, vendors and long-term architecture.
